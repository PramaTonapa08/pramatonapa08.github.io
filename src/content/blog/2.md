When I first started writing code alongside my statistics and math coursework, I thought programming and probability theory lived in two completely separate worlds. One was about syntax, loops, and getting a terminal to stop throwing red text at you. The other was about distributions, integrals, and proving why a theorem holds.

After enough late nights staring at broken scripts, I realized they are basically the exact same thing. Debugging is just applied hypothesis testing—except the p-value is your patience and the sample size is how many `print()` statements you are willing to scatter across your file.

## Formulating the null hypothesis

Every bug starts with an assumption. You write a function, run it, and get an output that makes zero sense. At that point, your null hypothesis ($H_0$) is simple: *"My logic is fine, and the input data is the problem."*

So you inspect the data. The data looks clean. You reject $H_0$ and move to the alternative hypothesis ($H_1$): *"I forgot an edge case in a loop three files away."* Just like in statistics, the hardest part isn't running the test—it's figuring out which variable you failed to control for in the first place.

## The illusion of deterministic math

In pure mathematics, if you follow the rules, you arrive at a clean, exact answer. An equation doesn't suddenly break because of a missing comma or a version mismatch in a library you installed three weeks ago.

Coding humbles you quickly because it introduces environment noise into pure logic. You can have the right mathematical formula for a simulation or a regression model, yet still get `NaN` across your entire output array because of a single division by zero hidden in row 4,012. Theory gives you the blueprint, but implementation forces you to deal with every messy edge case reality can throw at you.

## Embracing the messy middle

There is a specific kind of satisfaction when a script finally runs cleanly after an hour of tracing a silent logic error. It is the exact same feeling as finally cracking a tough proof after filling three pages of scratch paper with dead ends.

Whether it is math, statistics, or software, the real skill isn't knowing all the answers upfront. It is learning how to stay calm when things break, isolate the variables, and systematically narrow down where your assumptions went wrong.
